Car Affordability Rules, Compared

You'll be told to spend 35% of your income, or 10% of gross monthly, or half your salary, or one year's salary. These are quoted as if they're interchangeable. On the same income they land nearly four times apart. Here's what each one actually says for you, and which is worth following.

Why They Disagree So Much

The rules aren't measuring the same thing, which is why quoting them side by side without saying so is misleading.

  • Some cap the payment, some cap the price. "10% of gross monthly" limits what leaves your account each month; "35% of annual income" limits the sticker. Those produce very different answers depending on your loan term and rate.
  • Some count all car costs, some only the loan. Insurance, fuel and maintenance run $300–450 a month for most drivers. A rule that includes them is far stricter than one that doesn't.
  • Some assume financing, some assume cash. The salary-multiple rules — half your salary, one year's salary — were written for outright purchases. Applying them to a financed car ignores interest entirely.
  • They were written for different eras. "One year's salary" dates from when the average new car cost a fraction of what it does now. It's the loosest rule on the list and the least useful today.

Which One to Actually Use

If you're financing, 20/4/10 is the one worth following: 20% down, no more than four years, and total car costs — payment plus insurance plus fuel plus maintenance — under 10% of gross income. It's the strictest of the common rules and it's strict for a reason. The 20% down payment keeps you from going underwater, the four-year term stops you paying interest on a depreciating asset for most of a decade, and the all-in cap prevents the classic mistake of budgeting for the payment and forgetting the insurance.

If you're paying cash, the salary-multiple rules make more sense, and half your annual salary is the sensible ceiling for most people. One year's salary is defensible only if you have no other debt, a full emergency fund, and you intend to keep the car a very long time.

The 35% rule sits in the middle and works reasonably as a quick sanity check on the sticker price before you look at financing at all.

What None of Them Include

Every rule here is built on income alone, which is their common weakness. They don't ask about:

  • Your housing cost. Someone paying 40% of income in rent cannot afford what these rules permit.
  • Other debt. Student loans and credit cards compete for the same money.
  • Your emergency fund. A large down payment that empties your savings trades one risk for a worse one.
  • Job stability. A four-year commitment on unstable income is a different proposition.
  • How long you'll keep it. Spending more on a car you'll own twelve years is very different from the same spend on a three-year trade cycle.

Treat whichever rule you pick as an upper bound, then subtract for anything above that applies to you.
