Why Lease Mileage Caps Are the Hidden Trap

A lease payment often looks like the cheapest option on paper — until you account for the mileage cap. Standard leases cap you at 10,000–12,000 miles per year, with overage fees typically running $0.20–$0.30 per mile beyond that. A full-time rideshare or delivery driver easily covers 35,000–45,000 miles a year, which can turn an apparently cheap lease payment into the most expensive option once overage fees are included — exactly what this calculator's lease overage figure is designed to surface before you sign anything.

Buy vs Rent vs Lease: The Real Trade-Offs

  • Buy usually wins on cost-per-mile for full-time, high-mileage drivers — no mileage cap, and you build equity in a vehicle you'll likely need to replace anyway due to wear.
  • Rent (Uber Xchange, HyreCar, Lyft Express Drive) costs more per mile but requires no loan approval, no long-term commitment, and usually bundles insurance and maintenance — a reasonable way to start driving before committing to a purchase.
  • Lease rarely works well for full-time high-mileage driving once overage fees are included, though it can make sense for part-time drivers who stay under the mileage cap.

The Loan Contract Risk Almost Nobody Checks

Most standard auto loan agreements include language restricting the vehicle from being used "for hire," in "livery service," or for other commercial purposes without the lender's written consent. Rideshare and delivery driving technically fall under that restriction at many lenders, even though it's rarely enforced day-to-day. The risk isn't hypothetical — if something goes wrong (an accident, a dispute, a loan review) and the lender discovers commercial use that wasn't disclosed, it can be treated as a contract violation. Before you start driving for hire in a financed vehicle, read your loan agreement's use restrictions, and if it's unclear, ask the lender directly in writing. A small number of lenders specifically cater to rideshare drivers and permit commercial use explicitly — refinancing to one of them removes this risk entirely.

What This Calculator Doesn't Account For

It doesn't include EV-specific charging costs (see our EV charging cost calculator if you're considering an electric vehicle), platform-specific EV bonuses some cities offer, or the standard mileage tax deduction, which can meaningfully offset your real after-tax cost regardless of which option you choose. It also doesn't account for faster depreciation on a high-mileage vehicle if you plan to sell it — budget for a shorter useful life than a typical personal-use car.
