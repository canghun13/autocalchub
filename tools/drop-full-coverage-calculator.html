Drop Full Coverage Calculator: Is Collision Still Worth It?

Collision and comprehensive coverage only ever pay up to what your car is worth, minus the deductible. As the car ages, that ceiling falls every year while the premium usually doesn't. ⚠️ A rule of thumb, not advice for your policy. The 10x test is the Insurance Information Institute's guideline: if the car is worth less than ten times a coverage's yearly premium, that coverage may not be worth it. "Average yearly payout" uses HLDI's average claim frequencies (6.1 collision and 8.0 comprehensive claims per 100 insured vehicles a year) and average claim costs, capped at your car's value; those figures come from newer vehicles, and your own odds depend on how and where you drive. Dropping collision never affects liability coverage, which your state requires.

Why the Math Changes Every Year

Collision and comprehensive pay the car's actual cash value, minus your deductible — never more. A $4,500 car with a $1,000 collision deductible can return at most $3,500, however bad the crash. Meanwhile the premium for that coverage stays roughly the same as the car loses 10% to 15% of its value each year. At some point you're paying hundreds a year to protect a few thousand dollars.

The 10x Rule, Applied to Each Coverage

The Insurance Information Institute's rule of thumb, cited by Kelley Blue Book: if your car is worth less than ten times the annual premium, the coverage may not be cost-effective. Run it separately for each coverage. Comprehensive — theft, hail, flood, fire, a deer — usually costs about half as much as collision, so it often stays worthwhile for several more years. Dropping collision alone is the most common first step.

The Question the Math Can't Answer

Insurance loses money on average for the buyer; that's how insurers stay in business. You buy it for the year you can't absorb the loss. If wrecking the car tomorrow would leave you unable to get to work, keep the coverage until you've saved enough to replace the car. If you could buy another car with cash, the premium is mostly a fee for a risk you can already carry.

Before You Drop Anything

  • Raise the deductible first. Going from $500 to $1,000 often cuts the collision premium noticeably and keeps you covered for a total loss.
  • Keep the liability limits high. Dropping collision saves money; cutting liability to the state minimum can cost you far more than the car is worth.
  • Check for uninsured motorist property damage coverage, which in some states covers your car when an uninsured driver hits you, for much less than collision.
  • Bank the savings. Putting the dropped premium into a car fund each month turns it into self-insurance.
