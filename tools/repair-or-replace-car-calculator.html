Why the 50% Rule Gets This Wrong

The rule you'll find everywhere says that if a repair costs more than half the car's value, replace it. It's easy to apply and it's missing the other half of the equation: a replacement has a cost too, and that cost varies by a factor of three depending on what you buy.

Take a $4,500 car needing a $3,200 transmission. That's 71% of value, so the 50% rule says replace without hesitation. Run the actual money over three years and the picture splits in two. Against a $28,000 new car, keeping the old one costs about $277 a month less. Against a $13,000 used car, replacing costs about $5 a month less — essentially a tie.

Same car, same repair, opposite answers. The variable that decided it was never in the 50% rule at all.

What This Calculator Counts

Cost per month, over a horizon you choose, for both paths:

  • Keeping it: the repair, further repairs you expect, insurance and registration, plus the value the car keeps losing while you own it — offset by what it's still worth at the end.
  • Replacing it: the old car you give up, loan payments made, higher running costs, minus the equity you've built in the replacement (its value less what you still owe).

Counting equity on both sides matters. A common mistake is comparing a repair bill against a monthly payment as though the payment buys nothing — but part of each payment becomes ownership. Equally, the repaired car isn't worthless at the end of the horizon.

Where Each Answer Tends to Win

Keeping usually wins when…

  • You'd be replacing with something new or near-new. First-year depreciation on a new car frequently exceeds the entire repair bill.
  • The repair is a one-off on an otherwise sound car — a water pump on a well-maintained vehicle, not the fourth failure this year.
  • You own the car outright and would need to finance the replacement.
  • The repair comes with a warranty. A remanufactured transmission with three years of coverage buys real certainty.

Replacing usually wins when…

  • The replacement is a modest used car rather than a new one.
  • Repairs are becoming a pattern. Once the annual repair bill climbs past roughly $2,500, keeping gets expensive fast.
  • The repair only buys you a short reprieve — a big bill on a car with other systems near failure.
  • Rust, frame damage, or a failing second major system means you're funding a decline rather than a fix.

The Things Money Can't Settle

The calculation above is honest about dollars and silent on everything else. A car that strands you costs more than its repair bill if you lose work over it. A repair that fixes a symptom on a car with three other worn systems isn't really a fix. And if you can absorb a $3,000 repair from savings but a $300 monthly payment would strain your budget every month for four years, the cheaper-per-month option may still be the wrong one for you.

Get a second quote before any repair over about $1,500, and ask the mechanic to split the estimate into what must be fixed now and what can wait. That split tells you whether you're buying one more year or five.
