Why Driving Usually Wins on Cash — and Loses on Time

Run the cash costs alone and driving beats shipping on almost every route. A 1,500-mile trip in a 30 MPG car costs roughly $155 in fuel, and even with three hotel nights and four days of meals you're around $815. Shipping the same car runs about $1,250 plus a flight. On paper it isn't close.

The reason people ship anyway is that driving 1,500 miles costs you about 24 hours behind the wheel spread over four days. Price those days at anything at all and the comparison flips. At $25 an hour the breakeven lands near 1,500 miles; at $40 an hour it drops to about 1,000; at $15 an hour you'd have to be going past 2,000 miles before shipping makes sense. If your time is genuinely free — you wanted the road trip anyway, you have the days spare — driving stays cheaper right across the country.

That's the honest version of this decision, and it's why the input that matters most is the one transport companies never ask about.

How Auto Transport Pricing Actually Works

Most bookings go through a broker rather than the carrier itself. The broker posts your vehicle on a load board and drivers bid on it, which is why two quotes for the same route can differ by hundreds of dollars and why a "guaranteed" price sometimes moves before pickup.

A few patterns hold consistently:

  • Per-mile rates fall as distance grows. A 300-mile move can run well over $1.50 a mile; a cross-country haul drops toward $0.55–0.70. Short moves are the worst value.
  • Enclosed transport adds roughly 30–60%. Worth it for classics and high-value cars, rarely otherwise.
  • Flexibility is the biggest discount available. A three-to-five day pickup window typically saves 10–20% over demanding a specific date.
  • Season moves prices. Summer and the autumn snowbird migration are the expensive windows.
  • Major lanes are cheaper. Metro-to-metro on an interstate corridor draws more bidders than a rural pickup.

Costs People Leave Out of the Driving Side

The driving estimate people carry in their head is usually just fuel, which is why shipping looks expensive by comparison. The full list is longer:

  • Lodging and meals for every night the trip takes — the largest cash item on any multi-day drive.
  • Wear and mileage-driven depreciation. Adding 2,800 miles to the odometer measurably reduces what the car sells for. Ten to fifteen cents a mile is a conservative allowance once fuel is counted separately.
  • Time off work, if the days aren't already part of the move.
  • The risk of a breakdown far from home, which is a real consideration on an older vehicle.

Shipping has hidden costs too: you're without the car for anywhere from a few days to two weeks, delivery windows are approximate, and carriers generally limit personal items to about 100 pounds in the trunk, uninsured.

When the Answer Is Obvious Without Running Numbers

  • Ship it if the car is a classic, a non-runner, or worth enough that adding thousands of miles matters more than the transport fee.
  • Ship it if you're moving more vehicles than licensed drivers.
  • Drive it under about 500 miles. Short-haul transport is priced badly and you'd arrive faster yourself.
  • Drive it if the car is old enough that the transport cost is a large share of its value.
