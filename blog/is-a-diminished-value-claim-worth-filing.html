Is a Diminished Value Claim Worth Filing?

11 min read · Updated August 2026 · Written by AutoCalcHub Team
Advertisement · Google AdSense

Your car was repaired properly and looks fine. It's still worth less than it was, because the accident now sits on its history report and every future buyer will see it. Diminished value is that loss, and a diminished value claim is the attempt to recover it.

Plenty of sites will hand you a 17c calculator and a number. Far fewer tell you that most people asking the question aren't eligible to claim at all, or that the number the formula produces is often smaller than the appraisal needed to support it.

Quick answer: You generally can only recover diminished value from the at-fault driver's insurer, not your own — most policies exclude it on first-party claims. If you were at fault, your car was totaled, or the damage never got reported, there's usually nothing to claim. If you clear those gates, the 17c formula caps the payout at 10% of pre-accident value and cuts it hard for mileage, so a high-mileage car often produces a claim worth less than the $300–$400 appraisal that would support it.

Step 1: The Eligibility Gates (Where Most Claims Die)

Work through these before touching any calculator. Failing any one usually ends the matter.

  • Someone else must be at fault. This is the big one. Diminished value is normally recovered as a third-party claim against the other driver's property damage liability coverage. The large majority of personal auto policies exclude diminished value from first-party coverage, so if you caused the accident there's typically nothing to pursue. Georgia is the notable exception, where insurers must address diminished value on first-party claims following the State Farm v. Mabry litigation.
  • The car must not have been totaled. A total loss settlement already pays full pre-accident value, so there's no separate diminished value to recover.
  • The damage must appear on record. Diminished value comes from the accident being visible to future buyers. If nothing was reported to CARFAX or AutoCheck and no claim was filed, there's no documented loss.
  • The car needs meaningful value left. A vehicle over roughly ten years old or past 100,000 miles is already heavily discounted, and the formula reduces such claims to near nothing.
  • You must be within the filing window. These are governed by state statutes of limitation for property damage, commonly two to four years but varying widely. Check yours early.

State law also varies on whether diminished value is recoverable at all and on what evidence is required. A few states are openly hostile to these claims; others accept them routinely.

Step 2: What the 17c Formula Pays

Most insurers reach for the 17c formula, which came out of that same Georgia case. It runs in three steps:

  1. Take 10% of pre-accident value. This is a hard ceiling on the entire claim, applied regardless of how bad the damage was.
  2. Multiply by a damage severity factor: 1.00 for severe structural damage, 0.75 major, 0.50 moderate, 0.25 minor, 0.00 for no structural damage or replaced panels.
  3. Multiply by a mileage factor: 1.00 under 20,000 miles, 0.80 to 40,000, 0.60 to 60,000, 0.40 to 80,000, 0.20 to 100,000, and effectively nothing above that.

On a $28,000 vehicle, that produces:

Damage Level15,000 miles45,000 miles85,000 miles
Severe structural$2,800$1,680$560
Major$2,100$1,260$420
Moderate$1,400$840$280
Minor$700$420$140

Two things jump out. The mileage multiplier does most of the damage — the same severe-structural claim drops from $2,800 to $560 purely on the odometer. And the 10% cap means a catastrophically damaged $28,000 car can never produce more than $2,800 under this method, even though real-world buyers would discount it far more.

The Formula Is a Floor, Not a Ceiling

The 17c formula is not law. It's an insurer convention that produces a low, consistent number, and it double-counts mileage — the pre-accident value already reflects the odometer, then the formula penalises it again. Courts in several states have questioned it, particularly for luxury and low-production vehicles where actual market losses run well past 10%.

Treat the 17c output as the insurer's opening position. A market-based appraisal, or documented dealer trade-in differentials on your specific car with and without the accident history, is what moves a settlement above it.

Step 3: The Breakeven Nobody Runs

An independent appraisal to document your loss typically costs $300–$400. Insurers generally won't take an unsupported number seriously, so that cost is close to mandatory on any contested claim. Against the table above:

The pattern is consistent: diminished value claims are worth filing on newer, lower-mileage cars with structural damage, and rarely worth it otherwise. If your car is past 80,000 miles or the damage was cosmetic, the claim likely costs more to prove than it returns — and that's before your time.

Below roughly $500 of estimated loss, it's usually worth filing informally without an appraisal: submit your own comparables and see whether the adjuster settles. You lose nothing but time.

How to File If the Math Works

  1. Confirm the other driver's liability is established and get their claim number.
  2. Gather the repair documentation — the full estimate, supplements, and final invoice showing what was structural versus cosmetic.
  3. Pull your history report to confirm what future buyers will actually see.
  4. Get the appraisal if the estimated claim clears the breakeven comfortably. Use an appraiser licensed in your state.
  5. Submit in writing with a specific number and the evidence behind it. Expect a low first offer.
  6. Escalate if needed — your state insurance department takes complaints, and small claims court is a realistic route for amounts under the local limit without needing a lawyer.

Be wary of firms that offer to handle the claim for a percentage. On a $1,000 claim, a 35% contingency plus fees can leave you with less than filing it yourself.

Common Mistakes With Diminished Value Claims

  • Claiming against your own policy after an at-fault accident. Almost always excluded outside Georgia.
  • Accepting the 17c number as final. It's a conservative opening offer, not a legal standard.
  • Paying for an appraisal on a small claim. Run the breakeven first.
  • Filing after a total loss settlement. Pre-accident value was already paid.
  • Waiting too long. Property damage statutes of limitation apply and vary by state.

Diminished value rules, formulas, and filing deadlines differ significantly by state and by policy. Confirm your specific situation with your state's insurance department or a licensed appraiser before relying on these figures.
