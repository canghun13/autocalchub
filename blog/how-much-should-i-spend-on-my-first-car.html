How Much Should You Spend on Your First Car? (By Age, Salary and Credit)

10 min read · Updated October 2026 · Written by AutoCalcHub Team

Every "how much car can I afford" rule was written with an average buyer in mind: someone with a credit history, a decade of driving behind them, and insurance priced accordingly. A first-time buyer is none of those things, and the same rule applied to the same salary produces a noticeably smaller car.

Quick answer: Keep payment plus insurance under about 10% of gross income if you can, and under 15% at the absolute most. For a first-time buyer that means far less car than the same salary buys an older driver, because young-driver insurance runs $151 a month at 22 and $330 at 18 (against about $102 at 40), and a thin credit file can push a used-car loan from 8.81% to 13.93%. At 22 on a $40,000 salary with no credit history, the 10% rule supports roughly a $7,400 car; at 15%, about $14,200. A 40-year-old with good credit on the same pay could spend about $3,000 to $3,600 more.

Why a First Car Costs More Than the Rules Assume

Two costs land on first-time buyers that the standard rules quietly assume away.

Insurance. Average premiums fall steeply with age: about $330 a month at 18, $207 at 20, $151 at 22, $119 at 25, and roughly $102 by 40. Because the most common budgeting rules cap payment plus insurance together, every extra dollar of premium is a dollar that can't go toward the payment. And if you finance, the lender will require full coverage, which costs more than the bare-minimum policies some of those averages include.

Interest. Lenders price a short or empty credit file like a weak one. Experian's Q2 2026 averages put used-car loans at 8.81% for prime borrowers (661–780), 13.93% for near-prime (601–660) and 19.10% for subprime. On a $15,000 loan over four years, that is the difference between about $2,850 of interest at prime and $4,650 at near-prime — or $6,570 at subprime.

What Your Salary Actually Buys, at 22

The gap in the last column — roughly $2,600 to $3,600 of car at the 10% rule — is the first-car penalty: same income, same rule, less vehicle, purely because of age and credit history.

At 18 the arithmetic breaks entirely. On a $40,000 salary the 10% cap is $333 a month, and the average 18-year-old premium is $330. There is nothing left for a payment. This is why most 18-year-olds who drive either stay on a parent's policy, buy a modest car outright, or accept a budget well past 10%.

Is $32,000 Too Much for a First Car?

It comes up often enough to answer directly. At 22, with 10% down over 48 months at a near-prime 13.93%, a $32,000 car carries a payment of about $786. Add $151 of insurance and you are at $937 a month. Under the 10% rule that needs a salary of about $112,000; even at the 15% ceiling it needs about $75,000.

Good credit helps less than people expect: at prime rates the payment falls to about $714, and the 10% salary requirement only drops to around $104,000. For most first-time buyers, a $32,000 car is a stretch the numbers don't support.

Cash, Loan, or Somewhere in Between?

First-time buyers face a choice the averages don't: whether to finance at all.

  • A cash car in the $5,000 to $10,000 range avoids the thin-file interest rate entirely and lets you skip full-coverage requirements if the car is old enough that liability-only makes sense. The trade-off is reliability risk, so budget $1,000 to $1,500 for repairs in year one.
  • A small loan you pay on time builds the credit history that makes the next car cheaper. Twelve to eighteen months of on-time payments can move a borrower from near-prime toward prime, which is worth thousands on a future loan.
  • A cosigner with good credit can bring the rate down sharply, but they are fully liable for the debt and the loan sits on their credit report. Our auto loan cosigner calculator shows what the lower rate is actually worth.

Get Two Quotes Before You Shop

The two numbers that decide a first-car budget are both knowable before you set foot on a lot:

  1. An insurance quote on the specific models you're considering. Premiums for young drivers vary widely by vehicle — sporty trims and high-theft models can cost far more than a sedan at the same price.
  2. A loan pre-approval. It tells you your actual rate instead of the average, and gives you a number the dealer has to beat. Credit unions are often more willing than big banks to lend to borrowers with a short history.

The Costs Nobody Mentions on a First Car

  • Registration, title and sales tax. Often several hundred to over a thousand dollars at purchase, due up front or rolled into the loan.
  • Gas. At $4.48 a gallon in September 2026, a 25 mpg car driven 1,000 miles a month costs about $180 in fuel.
  • Maintenance and repairs. A used car's first year commonly needs tires, brakes or fluids — worth budgeting even if nothing breaks.
  • Parking. In cities and on campuses this can rival the insurance bill.

Payment plus insurance under 10% of gross leaves room for these. Payment plus insurance at 15% often doesn't.

Common First-Car Mistakes

  • Budgeting the payment and forgetting the premium. At 22 the insurance can be a third of the total.
  • Taking a 72- or 84-month loan to make a payment fit. On a thin-file rate this multiplies the interest and leaves you owing more than the car is worth for years.
  • Shopping by payment at the dealership. Negotiate the price, then the financing — never "what monthly payment are you looking for?"
  • Skipping the pre-approval. Without one, you have no way to know whether the rate you're offered is fair.
  • Buying the car you'll want in five years. The first car's job is to be reliable and cheap while your income and credit catch up.
