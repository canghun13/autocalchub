Do You Actually Need a Truck?

10 min read · Updated September 2026 · Written by AutoCalcHub Team
Advertisement · Google AdSense

You've been looking at pickups. The reasoning is usually some version of: I move stuff a few times a year, I've had to borrow my brother-in-law's truck twice, and it would just be easier to have one. All of that is true. None of it is a number.

Quick answer: A half-ton pickup typically costs about $231 a month more than the crossover you'd otherwise buy — roughly $13,900 over five years, once you count fuel, insurance, registration and the bigger depreciation. At $129 for a rental day, that premium buys about 107 rental days, or 21 a year. If you haul six or twelve times a year, renting wins comfortably. If you tow anything, owning usually wins regardless of the arithmetic.

The gap is $13,860 over five years. Note the fuel line in isolation: $625 a year, or $3,127 over five years, that you spend before hauling anything at all. That's the price of capability sitting idle in the driveway.

What $13,860 Buys in Rentals

Home Depot rents pickups and cargo vans at roughly $19 for 75 minutes or about $129 for a full day, plus $5 per extra fifteen minutes and a deposit. U-Haul is comparable. Store delivery on furniture and building materials generally runs $79–99 and is often waived above a spend threshold.

Twenty-one full rental days a year is the break-even. That's roughly every other weekend, all year, for five years. Most people who feel they need a truck haul something six to twelve times a year, and a good half of those are 75-minute runs that cost $19.

Worth being precise about the honest version of this: renting doesn't beat owning because renting is clever. It wins because most hauling is infrequent and short, and a truck charges you continuously for a capability you use intermittently.

Count Last Year, Not the Year You Imagine

The number that decides this is how often you haul, and it's the number people get most wrong. The truck you're picturing is being justified by the projects you intend to start.

A more reliable method: go through last year's calendar, photos and receipts and count the actual times you moved something that wouldn't fit in a car. Then split the list into hauls that genuinely needed a truck bed and ones where delivery, a roof rack, folded seats or a small trailer would have worked. Most lists shrink by half on the second pass.

If the honest figure comes out above twenty a year, buy the truck — the math supports you. Between about ten and twenty it's close enough that convenience reasonably decides. Below ten, you're paying $231 a month for something you use nine times.

When Owning Genuinely Wins

  • You tow. This is the big one. Rental pickups typically aren't equipped for towing, and rented tow vehicles are hard to find. A boat, camper, or equipment trailer makes ownership close to mandatory.
  • You haul for work. Weekly or daily use clears the break-even easily, and business use may be deductible.
  • Your hauling is unplanned. Rentals depend on availability and opening hours. If you need it at 6am on a Sunday, that has real value.
  • You're far from a rental location. Forty minutes each way to collect a truck erases the savings on short jobs.
  • Loads that don't rent well. Livestock, dirty or hazardous materials, anything that would earn a cleaning fee.
  • Weather and terrain. Rural, unpaved, or heavy-snow driving where the capability is a daily benefit rather than an occasional one.

The Middle Options People Skip

The choice isn't binary, and the in-between options are where most of the value sits.

  • A mid-size truck. Narrows both the price and fuel gaps substantially while still handling most bed-hauling. Often the right answer for someone who genuinely hauls but not constantly.
  • A trailer behind an SUV. A utility trailer costs a fraction of a truck and covers a surprising share of loads — provided you stay inside your vehicle's tow and payload ratings, which are lower than most owners assume.
  • An older truck bought outright. A $9,000 second truck for occasional use can cost less overall than the premium on a new one, though you'll pay insurance and registration twice.
  • Delivery. This covers the single most common trigger for truck-buying: getting one large purchase home, once.

What the Numbers Can't Settle

Cost isn't the only input, and pretending it is would be its own kind of dishonesty. A truck you enjoy driving, that fits how you see yourself, that means you never have to ask a favour again — those are real. The point of running the numbers isn't to talk you out of the truck. It's so that when you buy it, you know you're paying about $231 a month for convenience and preference, and you've decided that's worth it.

That's a completely defensible decision. "I didn't realise it cost that much" is the one worth avoiding.

Common Mistakes With This Decision

  • Comparing sticker prices only. Fuel, insurance and depreciation are the larger part of the gap.
  • Counting hauls you intend rather than hauls you made. Use last year's actual record.
  • Forgetting the 75-minute rate. Many trips cost $19, not $129, which pushes the break-even much higher.
  • Assuming an SUV can't tow. Many tow 3,500 lbs or more, which covers a small utility or boat trailer.
  • Ignoring the towing exception. If you tow, most of the above stops applying.
