Is Full Coverage Worth It on an Older Car? The Math by Car Value

8 min read · Updated November 2026 · Written by AutoCalcHub Team

"Full coverage" isn't a single product. It's liability, which your state requires, plus two optional coverages for your own car: collision and comprehensive. On a new car those two are essential. On a ten-year-old car they can cost hundreds a year to protect a payout that keeps getting smaller. Here's how to tell where your car sits.

Quick answer: Use the Insurance Information Institute's 10x rule on each coverage: if your car is worth less than ten times that coverage's yearly premium, consider dropping it. At 2024's national averages — $516 a year for collision and $283 for comprehensive — collision fails the test once the car is worth less than about $5,200, and comprehensive below about $2,800. Drop it only if the car is paid off and you could replace it from savings. Keep your liability limits high either way.

What Collision and Comprehensive Can Actually Pay

Both coverages pay the car's actual cash value — what it's worth on the day of the loss — minus your deductible. Never more. Collision covers crashes, whoever's at fault. Comprehensive covers theft, hail, flood, fire, vandalism and hitting a deer.

That ceiling is the whole story. A car worth $4,000 with a $1,000 collision deductible can return at most $3,000 from collision, even if it's totaled. As the car loses 10% to 15% a year, the ceiling falls with it. The premium usually doesn't.

The 10x Rule, Coverage by Coverage

Kelley Blue Book cites the Insurance Information Institute's guideline: if the car is worth less than ten times the annual premium, the coverage may not be cost-effective. Apply it to each coverage separately, because comprehensive is usually much cheaper than collision and often stays worthwhile for years after collision stops making sense.

Using the NAIC's 2024 national averages, with a $1,000 collision deductible and a $500 comprehensive deductible:

Your own premiums may be far from the averages — young drivers, cities and some states pay much more — so use the numbers on your declarations page. A higher premium moves the line up: at $900 a year for collision, it fails below $9,000.

How Often Drivers Actually Claim

Insurance data puts the odds in perspective. The Highway Loss Data Institute counts about 6.1 collision claims and 8.0 comprehensive claims for every 100 insured vehicles a year — roughly one collision claim every 16 years and one comprehensive claim every 12, for an average car. Average claim costs on recent models run about $8,700 for collision and $2,400 for comprehensive, but on an older car the payout is capped at its value.

On a $4,000 car, that works out to an average of roughly $180 a year in collision payouts against $516 in premiums. That gap is normal — insurers pay out less than they collect — but on an older car it grows large, while what you're protecting shrinks.

When Dropping Makes Sense — and When It Doesn't

  • The car must be paid off. Lenders and leasing companies require both coverages until the loan or lease ends.
  • You need to be able to replace it. Dropping collision means a crash you cause leaves you with nothing for the car. If losing it would cost you your job because you couldn't buy another one, keep the coverage until you've saved its value.
  • Drop collision first. It's the expensive one, and it's the one that stops making sense earlier. Comprehensive is cheap protection against theft and weather.
  • Never cut liability to save money. Liability protects you from lawsuits after you injure someone; its cost has nothing to do with your car's value.

A Middle Ground: Raise the Deductible

If the car is borderline, moving the collision deductible from $500 to $1,000 lowers the premium and still protects you against a total loss — the event that would really hurt. It's a reasonable step for a car worth $5,000 to $8,000 while you build savings.

When Your Car Crosses the Line

Depreciation moves the answer for you. A car worth $10,000 today, losing 15% a year, falls below $5,200 — the 10x line at average collision premiums — in about five years. Set a reminder to rerun the numbers at each renewal; the year the car crosses the line is the year the premium stops paying for itself.
