How Much Can My SUV Actually Tow?

10 min read · Updated September 2026 · Written by AutoCalcHub Team
Advertisement · Google AdSense

The brochure says 5,000 pounds. The trailer you're looking at weighs 3,500. That seems settled, and it usually isn't — because the advertised number assumes an empty vehicle with one person in it, and you are unlikely to tow that way.

Quick answer: Your real towing limit is whichever comes first: the tow rating, or what's left of your payload after passengers, cargo and the trailer's tongue weight. A mid-size SUV rated at 5,000 lbs drops to about 2,667 lbs once four people and camping gear are aboard. A pickup rated at 11,000 lbs falls to roughly 5,000 lbs with five people and equipment. Payload runs out long before the tow rating does.

Four Numbers, and Which Ones Actually Bind

The terminology is the reason people get this wrong, so here it is in plain terms:

  • Curb weight — what the vehicle weighs empty, with fuel, no people or cargo.
  • GVWR (Gross Vehicle Weight Rating) — the most the vehicle itself may weigh, fully loaded. Payload = GVWR − curb weight.
  • GCWR (Gross Combined Weight Rating) — the most the vehicle and trailer may weigh together.
  • Tow rating — the advertised maximum trailer weight, derived from GCWR under ideal conditions.

The trap is that tongue weight counts against payload, not against the tow rating. A trailer presses down on the hitch at typically 10–15% of its total weight, and that force sits on your rear axle exactly as if you'd loaded it into the cargo area. So every passenger, every cooler, and every bag competes with the trailer for the same allowance.

An 11,000 lb rating becomes 5,000 lbs with a full cab. This is why half-ton owners are so often surprised at the scales, and why the phrase "payload is the limit, not towing" gets repeated by everyone who tows regularly.

Where to Find Your Actual Numbers

  1. The yellow sticker on the driver's door jamb gives your vehicle's specific payload — "The combined weight of occupants and cargo should never exceed X lbs." This is your truck, not the brochure's.
  2. GVWR is on the same door jamb label.
  3. Tow rating is VIN-specific and varies by engine, axle ratio, cab configuration and whether the tow package was fitted. The number in the advertisement is usually the best possible combination.
  4. Weigh the trailer loaded, not empty. A trailer's advertised dry weight excludes water, propane, gear and everything you'll actually bring. Public scales are cheap.

The 80% Rule

Experienced towers generally stay at or below 80% of the rated capacity. It isn't a legal requirement; it's margin for hills, headwinds, braking distance and the fact that your weight estimates are probably optimistic.

Rated capacityPractical ceiling at 80%
3,500 lbs2,800 lbs
5,000 lbs4,000 lbs

Towing at the rating on a flat test track is one thing. Towing at the rating over a mountain pass in summer heat with a family aboard is another, and it's where transmissions and brakes get destroyed.

What Else the Rating Assumes

  • The right hitch class. A hitch rated below your trailer weight is the limit, regardless of what the vehicle can do.
  • A weight-distributing hitch above roughly 5,000 lbs on most vehicles. Many ratings are only valid with one fitted.
  • Trailer brakes. Most states require them above a threshold, commonly 3,000 lbs, and your vehicle may need a controller installed.
  • The tow package. Transmission and engine oil coolers, a heavier hitch receiver and upgraded wiring. Without it, the rating is often far lower.
  • Tyre load ratings. Occasionally the binding constraint on heavily loaded vehicles.

Common Mistakes When Towing

  • Using the advertised tow rating for your trim. It's VIN-specific and the advert quotes the best configuration.
  • Forgetting tongue weight eats payload. The single most common error, and the one that puts people over GVWR without knowing.
  • Using the trailer's dry weight. Water, propane and gear routinely add several hundred pounds.
  • Not counting passengers. Four adults is roughly 700 lbs of payload gone.
  • Towing at 100% of the rating. Legal, occasionally, but hard on the vehicle and unforgiving of error.

Ratings vary by trim, engine, axle ratio and equipment, and towing laws differ by state. Confirm your specific vehicle's numbers on the door-jamb label and in the owner's manual before towing anything.
