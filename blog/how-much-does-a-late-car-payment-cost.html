How Much Does a Late Car Payment Cost? (5, 15, 30 and 60 Days Late)

8 min read · Updated October 2026 · Written by AutoCalcHub Team
Advertisement · Google AdSense

Most people who are late on a car payment worry about the wrong thing. The interest is trivial and the late fee is annoying but small. The expensive part is a single date on the calendar: the day the payment becomes 30 days past due.

Quick answer: On a typical $520 payment, being 5 days late costs about 40 cents in extra interest and usually no fee. At 15 days, past most grace periods, add a late fee of $25 to $50 (or 3% to 5% of the payment). At 30 days, the lender can report the payment as late to the credit bureaus. In FICO's own example, that dropped a 793 score to the 710–730 range. Take a $25,000 to $30,000 car loan while the mark is fresh and the higher rate can cost $1,100 to $2,100. If you can't pay in full, everything you do should aim at staying under 30 days.

Grace periods are set by your contract, not by law. Ten to fifteen days is common, and some contracts have none. Your payment is technically late the day after the due date even inside a grace period; the grace period only decides when the fee kicks in.

The Interest: Smaller Than You Think

Almost all US auto loans charge simple interest by the day. When you pay late, the interest owed since your last payment keeps growing, so a bit more of your payment goes to interest and a bit less to the balance.

The amount is tiny. On a $22,000 balance at 8.5%, about $364 of a $520 payment would normally go to principal. Leaving that $364 on the loan for an extra 15 days costs about $1.27. Even a full month late adds only around $2.50 in interest. If you're agonizing over interest, don't. The fee and the credit report are what cost money.

The Late Fee

Your contract will state the fee in one of a few ways:

  • A flat amount, commonly $25 to $50 per late payment.
  • A percentage of the payment, commonly 3% to 5%. On $520, that's $15.60 to $26.
  • Whichever is greater (or occasionally whichever is less) of a flat fee and a percentage.

Some states cap auto loan late fees, either with a dollar ceiling or a percentage limit, and a few only require the fee to be "reasonable." Your retail installment contract will list the exact fee — look under late charges or default. Most contracts charge it once per late payment, but if you fall a second payment behind, you'll usually owe a second fee.

If this is your first late payment in years, call and ask for the fee to be waived. Many lenders do it once, and it costs you nothing to ask.

Day 30: Where It Gets Expensive

Lenders generally report a payment to Experian, Equifax and TransUnion once it is a full 30 days past due. Before then, being late is between you and your lender. After that, it's on your credit report for seven years.

How far your score falls depends on the rest of your file. FICO has published two examples of a single 30-day late payment:

What That Score Drop Costs on Your Next Car Loan

The score drop matters most when you apply for credit. Experian's Q2 2026 data puts the average new-car rate at 4.41% for super-prime borrowers and 6.15% for prime borrowers. Used-car rates run 6.29% and 8.81%, and climb to 13.93% for near-prime. Estimating rates between those tier averages, here's what one 30-day late can cost on a five-year loan taken while the mark is recent:

That's 35 to 70 times the late fee, from one payment that went a few days too long. The same applies if you try to refinance your current loan. And because many insurers use credit-based insurance scores in most states, a late payment can nudge your premium up at renewal too.

The effect fades. A single late payment from four years ago weighs much less than one from four months ago, especially if everything since has been on time. If you need a car loan or refinance soon, though, recent is exactly when it hurts.

60 and 90 Days: Default and Repossession

If the account rolls to 60 days, it's reported again with a worse status, and the score damage deepens. At 90 days, many lenders treat the loan as in default.

Repossession rules vary by state and by contract. In many states a lender can repossess as soon as you're in default, and some contracts define default as a single missed payment. In practice, most lenders wait until you're 60 to 90 days behind. Repossession adds towing, storage and sale costs. If the car sells at auction for less than you owe, you'll usually owe the difference too, and the repossession stays on your credit report for seven years.

If you can see that coming, selling the car yourself almost always leaves you in a better position than letting the lender take it.

How to Keep It Under 30 Days

  1. Call before the due date if you can. A due-date change or a deferment agreed in advance isn't reported as late. A payment you simply miss is. Our deferment calculator shows what a skipped month really costs in interest — usually far less than a 30-day late mark.
  2. If you're already late, pay before day 30, even if it means paying the late fee. A $30 fee is cheap insurance against a $2,000 rate increase.
  3. Pay what you can if you can't pay it all. It may not stop the fee or the report, but it can keep the account from rolling to 60 days.
  4. Set up autopay a few days after payday once you're caught up, so the problem doesn't repeat.
  5. After you're caught up, ask for a goodwill adjustment if it was a one-time slip. Lenders don't have to remove an accurate late payment, and many won't, but some will for a long-standing customer.

If the payment is too high every month rather than just this month, a short-term fix won't hold. Check whether a refinance could lower it. Do it before a late payment makes refinancing more expensive.
