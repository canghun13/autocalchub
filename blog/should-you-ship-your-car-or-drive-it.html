Should You Ship Your Car or Drive It?

9 min read · Updated August 2026 · Written by AutoCalcHub Team
Advertisement · Google AdSense

You're moving across the country and the car has to get there somehow. Transport companies will quote you a price in about thirty seconds. What none of them will do is price the alternative properly, which is why so many people ship without ever checking whether it made sense.

Quick answer: On cash alone, driving wins on almost every route — a 1,500-mile trip costs roughly $965 in fuel, hotels, meals and wear versus about $1,500 to ship plus a flight. The decision only flips when you price your own time. At $25 an hour the breakeven lands near 1,500 miles; at $40 an hour it drops to around 1,000; if your time is genuinely free, driving stays cheaper coast to coast.

The Comparison Most People Never Finish

The mental version of this decision is usually "shipping costs $1,200, gas costs $300, so driving is obviously cheaper." That's true as far as it goes and it leaves out most of the trip.

Here's a 1,500-mile move in a 30 MPG car at $3.10 a gallon, driving eight hours a day:

How Shipping Is Actually Priced

Most bookings go through a broker, not the carrier. The broker lists your vehicle on a load board and drivers bid on it. That auction structure explains almost every strange thing about auto transport quotes — why two companies quote hundreds apart for the same route, why a price can move before pickup, and why nobody will commit to an exact delivery date.

Consistent patterns worth knowing:

  • Short moves are terrible value. Under 500 miles you can pay $1.50 a mile or more, against $0.55–0.70 cross-country. The carrier's fixed costs get spread over fewer miles.
  • Flexibility is the real discount. A three-to-five day pickup window typically saves 10–20% over insisting on a date.
  • Enclosed carriers add 30–60%. Justified for classics and high-value cars; rarely otherwise.
  • Season matters. Summer and the autumn snowbird migration are peak. Late winter is cheapest.
  • Rural pickups cost more. Fewer carriers bid on loads off the main interstate corridors.
  • Non-runners add $150–300 because loading requires a winch.

The Costs Each Side Hides

What driving really costs

Beyond fuel, the honest list includes lodging and meals for every night on the road, plus wear and mileage-driven depreciation. Adding 2,800 miles to an odometer measurably reduces what the car sells for later — ten to fifteen cents a mile is a conservative allowance once fuel is counted separately. There's also the risk of a breakdown a thousand miles from home, which matters more on an older car.

What shipping really costs

You're without the vehicle for anywhere from a few days to two weeks, and delivery windows are estimates rather than appointments. Carriers typically cap personal items at about 100 pounds in the trunk, and those items aren't covered by the carrier's insurance. Door-to-door often means "as close as a car hauler can legally get," which may be a parking lot several miles away.

When You Don't Need the Calculator

  • Ship it if the car is a classic, a non-runner, or valuable enough that thousands of extra miles cost more than the transport fee.
  • Ship it if you're moving more cars than you have licensed drivers.
  • Drive it under 500 miles. Short-haul transport is priced badly and you'd get there sooner yourself.
  • Drive it if the car is old enough that shipping costs a meaningful fraction of what it's worth.

If You Do Ship: Practical Checks

  1. Get three quotes and treat wildly low ones with suspicion — a lowball bid that no driver accepts just means your car sits.
  2. Verify the company's USDOT/MC number and confirm whether you're talking to a broker or a carrier.
  3. Ask what insurance covers and get the carrier's certificate. Your own policy may or may not apply in transit.
  4. Photograph the car thoroughly before pickup, and check the Bill of Lading condition report carefully at both ends.
  5. Never pay a large deposit up front to a broker you haven't verified.

Common Mistakes With This Decision

  • Comparing shipping to fuel only. Hotels and meals usually cost more than the gas.
  • Valuing your time at zero without meaning to. If you're burning vacation days, they have a price.
  • Ignoring mileage depreciation. A cross-country drive adds real miles to a car you'll eventually sell.
  • Booking the cheapest quote. In a bidding market, the lowest number is often the one that doesn't get picked up.
  • Shipping short distances. Under 500 miles the per-mile economics work against you badly.
